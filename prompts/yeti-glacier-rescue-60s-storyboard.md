# Yeti Glacier Rescue 60s Storyboard

Babu Yeti is warming a lost arctic fox kit in a snow hollow at the foot of a glacier when a slab of ice as big as a house splits away above him. He curls over the kit to shield it, Mama Yeti slides into the hollow to shield them both, and Papa Yeti spins on a stone pillar and hurls a giant snowball that shatters the falling slab into a sky of glittering ice dust. The kit ends the film back beside its mother. The point is that the smallest one protects the smallest life, and his family protects him. It has been strictly formatted for AI video generation according to character styling rules, environment locks, cinematic direction, and social media prompt guidelines. There is no dialogue; the story is carried by gesture, expression, and sound.

This film is adapted from a 20-second source storyboard. The girl in the snow trench becomes Babu Yeti, the trench becomes a shallow snow hollow at ground level, the small creature on the stone pillar becomes Papa Yeti, and its fire beam becomes a thrown snowball. Mama Yeti's slide, the lost kit's mother, and the second half of the film are new.

## Agent instructions

This storyboard is six 10-second Google Flow videos that cut together into one 60-second film. Unlike the vertical Yeti shorts, this film is widescreen 16:9. The file holds the video prompts only: each video's table is the text prompt for one Flow generation. The images each clip is generated with, the video's 3x2 grid and its six panel images, are in `prompts/yeti-glacier-rescue-60s-3x2.md` under the same `## Video N - <Title>` heading, with the steps for generating and checking them. No clip starts or ends on a supplied frame.

Follow these steps in order.

1. Before generating a clip, generate its video's images and check them as `prompts/yeti-glacier-rescue-60s-3x2.md` says. Do not generate a clip without them.
2. Set Flow's output format to landscape 16:9 before generating any clip, and check it again before each one. A project last used for the vertical Yeti films keeps its 9:16 setting.
3. Generate Video 1 in Flow's ingredients-to-video mode. Attach the Video 1 images for the route you pick in the 3x2 file: the six panel images and the family reference image, which is seven images and the most Flow accepts for one clip, or the grid image and the family reference image. Paste the three Video 1 table rows in order as one prompt, VFX cells included. Do not generate the rows separately, and do not set a start or an end frame.
4. Check the clip: horizontal 16:9, and it matches its video's grid panels and panel images in pose, expression, colour, tuft, size, head, framing, and light, each at its timecode. No Yeti changes colour, grows or shrinks, or picks up clothing partway through, and none of the grid's label text appears. The camera moves the way each `Camera:` sentence says, and no effect appears that its `Effects:` sentence does not name. If it drifts, regenerate the clip before continuing; do not fix it in the edit.
5. Generate each following video the same way, with its own images and its own table as the prompt. Its first beat picks up the picture the video before it ended on, under the same light. Put the end of the previous clip beside the start of the new one and regenerate the new clip if the cut jumps in position, face, colour, or light. Repeat the checks in step 4 after every clip. All six videos take place in one valley, so there is no scene break.
6. Cut the six clips together in order with hard cuts and no other transitions. Keep the music continuous across the cuts. Apply one colour grade to the whole cut, matched to the palettes in the `Lighting lock:` paragraph, and keep the turn from cold blue-grey to warm gold where it happens, in the last shot of Video 4, rather than evening the two halves out. Do not retime any clip; each one stays exactly 10 seconds.

Every video's table runs on its own 00:00 - 00:10 clock. Paste one table into Flow at a time, and paste only the table. The `Film position:` and `Stage:` lines under each heading say where the clip sits in the finished 60-second cut and which stage it carries; they are for the operator and the editor, not for Flow. If Flow produces more than one clip, a clip longer than 10 seconds, or a timing error, the prompt contained timestamps above 00:10. Remove them and retry.

Every visual cell opens with `Horizontal 16:9 aspect ratio, full-frame widescreen composition.` Keep that line in the prompt even when Flow's output format is already set to 16:9. The wide valley shots, the whip pan in Video 4, and the break of the slab are staged across the width of the frame, and the setting and the line together keep them there. If a clip renders 9:16, regenerate it with the format reset rather than cropping, because cropping a vertical clip to widescreen throws away both sides of the valley.

Do not change the aspect ratio line, style lock, environment sentence, `Lighting:` sentences, head lock, look lock, scale lock, avoid line, character descriptions, or the `Animals:` sentence in any prompt. The images in `prompts/yeti-glacier-rescue-60s-3x2.md` were generated under that light and with those locks, so a clip prompt with different ones fights the images attached to it. Only the `Action:` text, the `Camera:` and VFX text, the audio text, and the expression phrase inside each character description (the words between "small round nose," or "small button nose," and the tuft of fur) differ between shots, and the expression phrase is already set for each shot to match its beat. Papa Yeti's rescue is a thrown snowball, a physical act. It is never a beam, a blast, or anything he casts, and nothing glows from any Yeti, because the copyright filter reads a Yeti power-up as a famous film moment. Keep it that way in any rewrite. The ice never touches a Yeti or a fox: the slab is stopped in the air, its fragments fly outward and away from the hollow, and only fine powder and ice dust reach the ground. If Flow rejects a clip, read the message. "This prompt may violate our policies" is the safety filter, and the cause is almost always a brand, a real device, a named stock sound effect, on-screen text, dialogue, or an effect that reads as harm (fire, a blast, a flash).
