# Papa Yeti and the Two Coins of Wisdom

## Cast

- **Papa Yeti**: a humble, honest father who spends his family's last two coins on wisdom and lives by it.
- **Mama Yeti**: a loving mother who worries about the empty bowls and shares the good news with everyone.
- **Babu Yeti**: their little son, who fetches the money box, waves Papa off, and welcomes him home.
- **Tashi Marmot**: the clever, rich neighbour next door, who grows jealous and accuses Papa Yeti.
- **Blue Magpie**: a sly stall-keeper on the pine road who sells Papa Yeti his first wisdom, meaning it as a trick.
- **Dolma Yak**: a kind old yak who keeps a tea stall at a high pass and sells him his second wisdom.
- **Grand Peacock**: three haughty, important-looking peacocks who pay Papa Yeti to bury a smelly bundle.
- **Snow Leopard Queen**: the ruler of the valley, who hears the truth and forgives.
- **Woolly Hare**: three friendly neighbours who come hopping to see the treasure.
- **Narrator**: the off-screen storyteller.

## Story

Once upon a time, in a mountain village, lived a kind Yeti family. Their little stone-and-timber cottage had a sagging slate roof, a woven bamboo door, and a tiny vegetable patch fenced with sticks. Next door lived Tashi Marmot, clever and very rich. Behind his whitewashed wall stood a grand painted house with a golden finial on its roof and a storehouse heaped with sacks of grain. One bright morning, Tashi Marmot stood by his storehouse, polishing a big gold coin on his crimson vest and holding it up to shine in the sun.

On the porch steps of the little cottage, Papa Yeti looked into the family's grain sack. It was nearly empty. Mama Yeti and Babu Yeti sat on the lowest step with their wooden bowls, and there were only a few grains of rice in each. Mama Yeti looked across at the grand house and sighed. "Look how rich Tashi is! Our bowls are nearly empty."

Papa Yeti rested a hand on his chest and said softly to himself, "She's right. I'm not so clever."

But then he lifted his head, and his eyes grew bright and determined. "I'll buy wisdom with our two coins!"

"Babu, fetch the money box!" said Mama Yeti with a gentle smile.

Babu Yeti hurried out through the bamboo door with the little carved wooden money box held against his tummy. "Here, Papa!" He lifted the lid. Inside lay exactly two copper coins, all the family had saved. Papa Yeti knelt down, and Babu Yeti tipped the coins into his big open hand.

Papa Yeti closed his hand around the coins and stood up tall. Mama Yeti and Babu Yeti waved from the porch. "Bye, Papa!" called Babu Yeti. And off he went, down the long mountain road.

The road wound up through a sunny pine forest. Under a crooked old pine stood a tiny stall with a striped awning, its counter covered in shiny trinkets, pebbles, buttons, and bits of coloured glass. No one seemed to be there, until Blue Magpie hopped up onto the counter and cocked its head.

Papa Yeti held out one copper coin. "Please, sell me some wisdom."

Blue Magpie eyed the coin and narrowed its eyes. Blue Magpie thought, I'll trick him! It fluffed its feathers, put on its grandest, most serious face, and raised one wing like a teacher. "Always do what respectable folk tell you."

Blue Magpie plucked the coin from Papa Yeti's hand and tucked it under its wing. Papa Yeti pressed a hand to his chest and beamed. "Thank you! I'll remember that." Then he walked on with a jaunty stride. He walked a long, long way.

At last the stony track climbed to a high, windswept mountain pass, where faded cloth flags snapped in the wind and a copper kettle steamed on a little clay stove. There, behind the counter of a small stone tea stall, stood Dolma Yak, an old yak with long shaggy hair and kind, half-closed eyes. Tired but steady, Papa Yeti held out his very last coin. "My last coin, for wisdom?"

Dolma Yak nodded slowly and nudged a cup of hot tea toward him. "Always tell the Queen the truth."

Papa Yeti smiled gently and rested a hand on his chest. He would remember that, too.

Further on, Papa Yeti found three grand peacocks. They stood at a meadow crossroads beside a little wooden handcart, around a long, lumpy bundle wrapped in white cloth with an old string knotted around it. Each Grand Peacock turned its beak away and fanned the air with one wing. "Phew! What a smelly old bundle!" said the middle one.

The Grand Peacock pointed a wing at the bundle and looked down its beak at Papa Yeti. "Bury it, and take these coins!" And it tipped three small silver coins into his hand.

Papa remembered the magpie's wisdom. These were very grand, important-looking folk. "Yes, of course!" he said brightly, and he lifted the wooden spade from beside the handcart.

The Grand Peacocks strutted away down the road with their noses in the air and never looked back. Papa Yeti dug and dug, tossing dark earth onto a growing heap, until a deep, neat pit lay open in the meadow grass. Then he leaned on his spade and let out a big, happy breath.

He bent down and lifted the bundle. It was surprisingly heavy. As he held it against his chest, the old string slipped loose and the white cloth fell open. Inside were sparkling jewels, gold necklaces, and piles of gold coins. But it wasn't rubbish at all. It was treasure!

"Jewels and gold!" gasped Papa Yeti, and his face broke into the biggest smile.

Papa Yeti carried the treasure all the way home. In the late-afternoon sun he set the bundle down on the porch step, and gold and jewels spilled across the worn wood. Mama Yeti pressed her hands to her chest. "Oh, Papa! How wonderful!" Babu Yeti lifted out a sparkling gold necklace and held it up with shining eyes. "It sparkles!"

Mama Yeti was so happy that she leaned over the stick fence and waved down the lane to the neighbours. "Papa found a treasure! Come and see!" Three Woolly Hares came hopping along, their ears straight up and their noses twitching. But next door, Tashi Marmot peeked over his whitewashed wall and scowled. Tashi Marmot grew terribly jealous.

He dropped down out of sight. Tashi Marmot hurried straight to the palace. While Papa Yeti sat on his porch, happily stacking gold coins into neat little piles, Tashi Marmot bowed low before Snow Leopard Queen in her grand hall of red pillars. "My neighbour took your treasure!"

The Queen narrowed her pale green eyes and raised one paw. "Bring him here!" Back at the cottage, three firm knocks sounded on the bamboo door, and Papa Yeti looked up from his coins, worried.

Papa Yeti walked slowly along the long red runner of the palace hall, his hands held together at his chest. A cloud passed over the sun, and the hall grew dim. Snow Leopard Queen leaned forward on her throne. "Did you take my treasure?"

Papa Yeti was afraid. Then Papa remembered the yak's wisdom. He lifted his chin, put a hand on his chest, and spoke clearly. "I'll tell you the whole truth."

Papa Yeti said, "I only did what the peacocks asked." He told her everything, from beginning to end. Snow Leopard Queen listened with her ears turned forward, and her stern eyes slowly softened. At the foot of the throne, Tashi Marmot shrank into his crimson vest, ashamed.

The sun came back through the lattice windows. The Queen sat back with a kind smile. "You are forgiven." Papa Yeti let out a long, relieved breath.

At sunset, the lantern by the cottage door was glowing when Papa Yeti came home along the golden lane. Babu Yeti ran and hugged his leg. "Papa's home!"

At the carved gate next door stood Tashi Marmot, all alone, his ears flat and his paws clasped. In a small voice he said, "I'm sorry I was jealous."

Papa Yeti turned and held out his open hand. "Come, Tashi. Share our supper." Tashi Marmot stepped out through his gate and put his small paw in Papa Yeti's big hand, and together they walked to the porch.

Mama Yeti set out a steaming pot of rice and a stack of wooden bowls with her warmest smile, and Babu Yeti handed Tashi Marmot a full bowl of his own. The family sat together on the porch steps as the sun went down and the first stars came out above the snowy peaks. And from that day on, they all lived happily.

An honest heart is the greatest treasure.

## Moral

An honest heart is the greatest treasure.

Long, long ago, in a small village, there lived two brothers named Dharamlal and Karamlal. The younger brother, Karamlal, was very clever and had earned a great deal of wealth. The elder brother, Dharamlal, however, was terribly poor.

One day, Dharamlal’s wife sighed and said to him, "Look at how rich your younger brother is! Meanwhile, it is hard for us to even manage enough food for our morning and evening meals."

Dharamlal thought to himself, "She is right. I am such a foolish fellow, and that is why we have to suffer through such hard days. My brother used his cleverness to earn a fortune and lives a life of comfort and luxury."

Determined to change his luck, Dharamlal asked his wife for the two rupees they had saved in their little wooden money box, and he set off down the road to buy some wisdom.

Along the way, he spotted a shop. He walked up to the shopkeeper, handed him one rupee, and said, "Please give me some wisdom."

The shopkeeper looked at the simple man and thought to himself, I should trick this foolish fellow. Taking the one rupee, he solemnly taught him a piece of wisdom: "Always obey what respectable people tell you to do."

Pleased with his purchase, Dharamlal tucked that advice into his heart and walked on. After traveling a very long way, he came across another shop and asked the shopkeeper to sell him a rupee's worth of wisdom with his last remaining coin. That shopkeeper also took the rupee and gave him this advice: "Always tell the truth in front of the King."

As Dharamlal continued down the road, he came upon a crowd. In the middle of the gathering, he saw several respectable gentlemen standing around a dead body. Seeing that Dharamlal looked like a simple, obedient man, they gave him a few rupees and ordered him, "Dig a hole and bury this corpse."

Right away, Dharamlal remembered his first piece of wisdom: Always obey what respectable people tell you to do. He immediately said, "Yes, of course!" and dug a deep pit right there.

Once the pit was dug, he lifted the body to place it inside. To his utter surprise, it wasn't a corpse at all—it was sparkling jewelry and piles of money wrapped up in a white cloth!

Dharamlal gathered up the treasure and carried it home. Seeing all the wealth he had brought back, his wife was overjoyed.

Excited by their good fortune, his wife told the neighbors all about the riches her husband had brought home. When his younger brother, Karamlal, saw that his elder brother had become even richer than he was, he grew terribly jealous. He went straight to the palace and complained to the King that his brother had stolen the King's treasure.

The King sent word for Dharamlal to appear at the royal court. Dharamlal went to the palace, stood before the throne, and used his second piece of wisdom. He told the King the whole truth from beginning to end. Seeing that Dharamlal had spoken with complete honesty right in front of him, the King forgave him, and Dharamlal lived happily for the rest of his days.
